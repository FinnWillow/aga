  # AGA: project brief
  **Another Generic Adventure: where plants have gone berserk and you need to figure out why!**
  Short: *AGA: Plants*, or just *AGA*. Remake of "Another Generic Adventure" (GMS 1.4)
  in GameMaker LTS 2026.

  ## Why this remake exists
  The original was my most complete game, but it had no sound, music, story or real
  gameplay. I kept building systems "so I could make the game later". When I finally
  tried to build a large level, the code couldn't run it, it lagged badly, and I lost
  the motivation. **Lesson: make the game fun first; systems serve the game, not the
  other way around.** Keep a sandbox test room for the whole project. Never delete it.

  ## The title
  - "Another Generic Adventure" is free on both Steam and itch.io.
  - The subtitle is the joke and the premise at once. It also works as a series
    template: e.g. "Another Generic Adventure: where zombies infect the lands and you
    need to figure out why, again!"

  ## Premise
  A forest deity has stayed passive for ages, and plants were passive because their
  ruler was. Humanity has now passed the point of no return on climate damage: even if
  everything stopped today, mass extinction is locked in. So the deity stopped waiting.
  Plants turn into monsters and kill humans. Example: the flower monster looks like a
  flower, then transforms when you get close.

  **Why now?** Because it's too late. Patience only made sense while there was
  something left to save.

  ## The spores
  - The deity also releases spores into the air to kill more humans. It's not as
    extreme as it sounds: about 50% of people are weak enough to die from them.
  - About 1% of the people who survive benefit from them instead, mostly people who
    live surrounded by plant matter, like forests. **Frost is one of them.**
  - What it gives them:
    - Eating plants, or animals that ate the spores, heals them. (Why food heals.)
    - Eating rare plant-monster organs gives them permanent abilities. (Why a human
      can use "magic".) Eating certain other things gives temporary boosts.
  - The cost: they are slowly turning into a plant monster themselves. Frost doesn't
    really care.
  - This is the Frost/deity mirror again, in their body this time: the deity's weapon
    against humanity is what keeps Frost alive.

  ## The protagonist: Frost
  - An adult who decided humans are crap and moved alone into the forest. They know
    living truly alone is hard; they don't care.
  - Takes no bullshit from anyone: people, life, or the forest. But they are
    **careful**, not reckless: they survive by reading a fight, picking their moment,
    and not taking stupid risks. The combat plays the same way.
  - **Gender and identity are never revealed.** The hat hides the eyes, including in
    any higher-res portrait. Who they are doesn't matter; what they do does.
  - **They make mistakes, fail, and live with the consequences.** Their flaws come from
    their own belief that they need nobody.
  - **Their choice to leave humanity is neither right nor wrong.** Maybe they could
    have saved lives; maybe they'd have died like everyone else. The game never
    answers this. Hint at both sides and let the player decide.
  - **They mirror the deity:** both judged humanity and turned away from it, one by
    staying out of it, the other by lashing out. Frost probably agrees with the
    deity's diagnosis and refuses to be one of its victims.

  ### Backstory: why Frost is a recluse
  - Frost used to have a normal life: friends, family, a partner, a well-paid job,
    little to be frustrated about.
  - After a family birthday reunion, the family drove home. Frost was in no condition
    to drive and walked home alone. They got home safely; the family of five died in a
    car crash.
  - Frost keeps asking whether it would have been different if they had been at the
    wheel, or had made sure someone sober drove. **This mirrors the game's central
    question** (could leaving humanity have cost lives?), and like that one, it is
    never answered.
  - The press covered it in the worst light ("Family of five die in a car crash! The
    only survivor walked home! Coincidence?"). There was a trial. Frost walked free,
    but couldn't live there any more.
  - It was a small town; the gossip spread. Within a week Frost lost the job, the
    partner, everything. They sold the house and, with their savings, built a house
    on the other side of the country, in **Morningfrost Woods**.
  - Their contempt for humanity is earned, not edgy: people judged them for
    something nobody could prove.

  ### The name
  - Frost never gave their name publicly. To pay for power, internet and water, they
    sell game and firewood to the nearby village.
  - The villagers called them "the recluse of Morningfrost", which became just
    **Frost**. Even their name comes from how others see them, not from who they are.
  - The villagers don't know the story. They are probably the only people who have
    treated Frost decently since the crash, and they are the first ones attacked.

  ## The opening (build late; needs most systems)
  - Starts as a normal day: cut down a tree (a bit like God of War), take the wood to
    the village and sell it. This teaches the controls (WASD, mouse, attacking,
    selling, entering buildings) and introduces the world and Frost.
  - After the sale, screaming in the streets: plants start attacking people. People
    run out of houses, a plant monster catches someone (saving them is optional:
    Frost's first "help or walk away" choice), fire, plants fleeing from the fire.
  - **Rule:** never "and then it happened". It is happening, and you are part of it.
  - The opening needs melee, shops, room transitions, NPCs, scripted events and fire,
    so it is built after the vertical slice, not as it.

  ## Setting and tone
  - Modern day, around 2016–2026. Starts looking Pokémon-like and cozy, then turns
    dangerous: pistols, shotguns, grenades, chainsaws, cars, and monsters that punish
    every mistake.
  - The world shows the damage: higher water levels, submerged cars and buildings,
    notes about extinct species, peoples and places erased from the map.
  - **Tone rule:** the fights are tense; the world is quietly tragic. Show the heavy
    material through environment and notes, never lecture. Frost reacts in a few dry
    words at most. (A submerged car with a child seat says more than any text.)

  ## Gameplay pillars
  1. **GBA/Pokémon-inspired top-down shooter for PC**: move with the keyboard, aim with
     the mouse.
  2. **Careful combat, souls-style:** dodge and punish. Recklessness is punished;
     exploring and taking on fights is rewarded. Aggressive play is possible (dashing
     through, shoving enemies into bad positions), but it has to be earned with skill.
  3. **Exploring and gathering:** most of the time is spent exploring, hunting and
     gathering (buildings, woods, forests, water), trading, and preparing. Driving
     when necessary.
  4. **Rare drops and crafting, Hypixel Skyblock-style:** enemies drop materials that
     are sold or turned into gear; rare drops give very strong equipment, either
     directly or as crafting materials. Never a sluggish grind: there are ways to raise
     your odds or narrow what you can get, so when you need something, you can work
     towards it.
  5. **Progression without levels:** no levelling system. You get stronger through
     equipment, weapon upgrades commissioned at traders, permanent abilities
     from plant-monster organs, and temporary boosts from food. Metroid-style ability gates open quests and extras.
  6. **Freedom:** the player can do what they want, including killing NPCs. Nothing is
     required. Frost's reactions, the world's reaction and the ending reflect the
     choices.
  7. **Journal:** the old bestiary book grows into Frost's journal: monster entries
     plus notes about the lost world.

  ## Combat
  - **Guns are the main weapon.** Melee is for making distance from an enemy, or
    finishing it off up close.
  - Three bars, souls-style:
    - **Health:** drains when hit. Getting hit also stuns you, which is what makes
      walking into a fight carelessly so dangerous. (A short invulnerability window
      after each hit, so groups can't stun-lock you to death.)
    - **Stamina:** spent by dodging *and* attacking, so you manage it between defence
      and attack. Leaning too hard either way is a slow but sure death.
    - **Exhaustion:** what abilities run on. Using abilities drains it; once it's
      empty, no abilities until you eat.
  - **Abilities** are permanent once unlocked (by eating a plant-monster organ) and
    can be toggled on and off.
  - **Food does the healing and refilling, like drinking Estus:** slow, and dangerous
    if done at the wrong moment. It's eaten from the inventory one item at a time, and
    the game doesn't stop while the inventory is open. Eating mid-fight is a risk you
    choose to take.
    - While eating, Frost can move, but slowly.
    - Getting hit mid-bite stops eating: you keep the share already restored, based
      on how far you got, and the item is spent anyway.
    - Unlike a single Estus flask, there are many foods: they differ in how much they
      restore and how many fit in a stack.
    - **Only stamina refills by itself.** Health and exhaustion never come back on
      their own, only from food, which restores them gradually while eating, not
      instantly. Some foods are better at clearing exhaustion than others.
    - Some foods refill stamina faster, for when you're in a pinch. These are usually
      instant, unless they also restore health or exhaustion.
    - Certain items give temporary boosts.

  ## Inventory and preparation
  - Weapons and tools are unlocks, not inventory items.
  - Stackable items (food, supplies, materials like wood and scrap metal, monster
    drops, ammo types) take one slot each, stacking up to ~99.
  - The inventory is small and storage on the go is expensive. Vehicles (bike,
    motorcycle, car) add some storage, still limited.
  - Constant selling and buying at different places.
  - Quests need preparation: some items must be carried to progress, otherwise you
    backtrack.

  ## Crafting
  - No crafting system of its own: **crafting is a commission at a trader.**
  - A recipe needs certain items (usually monster-plant parts), money, the right
    trader (some only exist in certain places), and time. Come back when it's done
    to get the unlock.
  - Example: an exotic plant-based weapon needs specific monster-plant parts, money, a
    special trader, and a wait.
  - The waiting runs on the game clock, and traders may keep schedules, so a
    commission is also about planning when to come back.
  - **Weapon upgrades are commissions too.** The weapon itself is part of what the
    trader asks for, so you can't use it until the upgrade is done.
  - Since time can be skipped, the waiting is never forced on you. It's a choice:
    spend the daylight (or the night) doing other things while it's being made, or
    skip ahead and get it sooner.

  ## Time
  - An in-game clock with a day/night cycle. The day length is decided by feel once
    it can be played (a Majora's Mask-like ~54 real minutes per day is the starting
    guess). Sleeping skips time; you are never forced to sleep.
  - **The world doesn't wait.** Some quests have a time limit, or have to be started at
    a certain place and time. Failing is not showing up on time, or not at all.
    Sleeping during a quest is fine, as long as you make it on time.
  - Some NPCs keep schedules (a farmer only sells first thing in the morning, and a bit
    at night). You learn them by talking to them, or by watching them.
  - Depending on what you do, NPCs can die, including while you're somewhere else.
    Off-screen events are not simulated: when it matters, the game checks what should
    have happened by now.

  ## Structure and story
  - **Nothing is required.** The deity can be reached from the first second you can
    move, and there's no time limit on it. You can go whenever you want, or never, and
    just live as a wandering merchant getting rich.
  - **The story is pieced together by the player:** from clues, notes, NPCs and Frost's
    reactions. If you don't care about it, you can skip it entirely.
  - Ability gates lock quests, items, abilities, boosts and lore, never the way to the
    deity.
  - Killing an NPC is a choice with no effect on the game's structure. What changes is
    how Frost reacts afterwards, how the world reacts to Frost, and the ending.
  - **How the world reacts** (the details depend on the story): prices go up, quests
    become unavailable, people attack Frost, and some even treat Frost as one of the
    plant monsters.

  ## The deity and the endings
  - The deity is a hideous amalgamation of many plant types.
  - Two choices, both valid to play:
    - **Kill it:** a very hard boss fight, beatable from the start if you have the
      skill (rush it at "level 1"; there are no levels). Ending: all plant life on
      Earth dies; all life follows within three years.
    - **Reason with it:** humanity and plants live in peace. Some plants keep walking
      the earth; others return to their passive state. Reasoning is earned: it only
      becomes possible once Frost understands the deity, through what was found while
      exploring. Rushing in gets you the fight.
  - **Full genocide**, UNDERTALE-style but only about humans: killing every human
    changes the game around it. The deity attacks on sight. It still speaks to Frost,
    but the player doesn't get to choose: Frost answers for themselves.
  - Talking with the deity is the Frost/deity mirror made literal: two beings who gave
    up on humanity, deciding whether there's another way.
  - The rush and the understanding requirement also give speedrunners two routes.

  ## Open decisions
  - Story beats and clues. To be figured out over time.
  - Music direction.
  - Day length: tune by feel once the clock exists.
  - Drop odds: how you raise your chances or narrow drops. Balance when there's gear
    that needs it.
  - What exactly counts as "understanding" the deity for the reason route.
  - The world's reaction in detail: tied to the story as it takes shape.
  - Ability costs: how much exhaustion each ability uses (per use, or while toggled on).
